Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

Perturb a system with a small sinusoid, measure the response, and divide. Repeat at a hundred frequencies and the quotient becomes a map of everything happening at the interface, sorted by how fast it happens. This tool builds that idea from the phasor up.

1.1A sinusoid is the shadow of a rotating vector

Take a vector of length Xo turning anticlockwise at a constant angular frequency ω. Project it onto the vertical axis and you get a sinusoid. One full turn, ωt = 2π, draws one complete cycle.

x(t)=Xosin⁡(ωt),ω=2πf,T=1fx(t) = X_o\sin(\omega t), \qquad \omega = 2\pi f, \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}

Three amplitudes name the same wave and are a standing source of confusion when reading a methods section. The peak Xo = Xp is what the equations above use; the peak-to-peak value is twice it; the root-mean-square value is what a multimeter reports.

Xpp=2Xo,Xrms=Xo2=0.707 XoX_{pp} = 2X_o, \qquad X_{rms} = \frac{X_o}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707\,X_o

1.2Two signals, one frequency, one phase angle

Apply a sinusoidal potential to a cell and the current that comes back is a sinusoid of the same frequency, differing only in amplitude and in phase. Freeze both rotating vectors at one instant and you have two phasors: arrows whose length is the amplitude and whose angle between them is the phase shift φ.

E(t)=ΔEsin⁡(ωt)⟶i(t)=Δisin⁡(ωt−φ)E(t) = \Delta E\sin(\omega t) \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad i(t) = \Delta i\sin(\omega t - \varphi)

The sign needs care. φ in that equation is the phase of the impedance, and it enters the current with a minus sign. So a negative φ, which is what every capacitive electrode gives, means the current reaches its maximum earlier than the potential: the current leads. Through a pure resistor φ = 0 and the two are in phase. The panels below are the same instant seen three ways; drag the phase and watch them move together.

1.3Why complex numbers make this easy

Carrying amplitudes and phase angles through a circuit by trigonometry is painful. Writing each phasor as a complex number turns the whole problem into arithmetic. A complex number has a rectangular form and a polar form, and Euler's relation connects them:

z=a+jb=∣z∣(cos⁡φ+jsin⁡φ)=∣z∣ ejφ,j2=−1z = a + jb = |z|\left(\cos\varphi + j\sin\varphi\right) = |z|\,e^{j\varphi}, \qquad j^2 = -1
∣z∣=a2+b2,φ=arctan⁡ ⁣(ba)|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}, \qquad \varphi = \arctan\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)

Electrochemists write j rather than i for the imaginary unit, because i is already the current. A sinusoid in the time domain becomes a single complex number in the frequency domain, and the common factor ejωt cancels when you take a ratio:

x(t)=Xocos⁡(ωt+φ)⟷X~=Xoe jφx(t) = X_o\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad \tilde{X} = X_o e^{\,j\varphi}

1.4Impedance is a transfer function

Any system that is linear and time-invariant, and that you interrogate with an input x(t), returns an output y(t) at the same frequency. The ratio of the two phasors is the transfer function, and when the input is potential and the output current, that transfer function is the impedance:

Z(ω)=E~I~=∣Z∣ e jφ=Z′+jZ′′Z(\omega) = \frac{\tilde{E}}{\tilde{I}} = |Z|\,e^{\,j\varphi} = Z' + jZ''
Z′=∣Z∣cos⁡φ,Z′′=∣Z∣sin⁡φ,∣Z∣=(Z′)2+(Z′′)2Z' = |Z|\cos\varphi, \qquad Z'' = |Z|\sin\varphi, \qquad |Z| = \sqrt{(Z')^2 + (Z'')^2}

Notice what the ratio has thrown away: the time, and the size of the perturbation. One frequency yields one complex number, that is, one point on a Nyquist plot. Sweeping the frequency traces the whole curve, and that is all an impedance spectrum is.

The word linear in that definition is a condition you have to arrange. The current–potential curve of an electrode is exponential, so only a small piece of it is locally straight. At 25 °C a 10 mV amplitude keeps the response linear to about one percent; 50 mV does not, the response acquires harmonics, and Z stops being well defined. The system must also be stationary, since the spectrum takes minutes to hours to record.

Three conditions, worth memorising. Linearity: the perturbation is small enough that the response is proportional to it. Causality: the response is produced only by your perturbation, not by something else in the room. Stability: the system is the same at the end of the sweep as at the start. The Kramers–Kronig test in §4.6 is the formal way to check that all three held.

2.1Three building blocks

Before any electrochemistry, learn to read the spectrum of a circuit made of ordinary components. Three passive elements cover almost everything.

ElementImpedanceZ′Z″PhaseWhat it does
Resistor RZ = RR00°opposes current, no delay, frequency-independent
Capacitor CZ = 1 / jωC0−1/ωC−90°stores charge; a short circuit when fast, an open circuit when slow
Inductor LZ = jωL0+ωL+90°opposes change of current; in EIS usually a cable artifact

The sign convention matters and trips people up. For a capacitor Z″ is negative, and Nyquist plots are drawn as −Z″ against Z′ so that capacitive behaviour appears in the upper half-plane. Inductive behaviour then appears below the axis.

Combining elements follows Kirchhoff's rules, with complex arithmetic doing the work of the trigonometry:

series: Zeq=Z1+Z2+⋯parallel: 1Zeq=1Z1+1Z2+⋯\text{series: } Z_{eq} = Z_1 + Z_2 + \cdots \qquad\qquad \text{parallel: } \frac{1}{Z_{eq}} = \frac{1}{Z_1} + \frac{1}{Z_2} + \cdots

In parallel it is often easier to add admittances, Y = 1/Z, which simply sum. Circuits are named with Boukamp's description code: elements in series are written next to each other and a parallel combination is enclosed in parentheses, so R(RC) is a resistor in series with a parallel resistor–capacitor pair.

2.3Reading the semicircle

The R(RC) circuit is worth knowing by heart, because tab 3 is mostly this circuit with physical names attached. Its impedance separates into

Z(ω)=R0+R11+(ωR1C1)2−j ωR12C11+(ωR1C1)2Z(\omega) = R_0 + \frac{R_1}{1+(\omega R_1C_1)^2} - j\,\frac{\omega R_1^2C_1}{1+(\omega R_1C_1)^2}

At high frequency the capacitor is a short circuit, so Z → R0: the left-hand intercept. At low frequency it is an open circuit and all the current goes through R1, so Z → R0 + R1: the right-hand intercept. The diameter of the semicircle is therefore R1, read off the real axis without any fitting.

Between the two, the imaginary part passes through a maximum exactly where the capacitive reactance equals the resistance. That frequency gives the time constant directly:

ωmax=1R1C1=1τ,fc=12πR1C1\omega_{max} = \frac{1}{R_1C_1} = \frac{1}{\tau}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R_1 C_1}

So a single semicircle hands you three numbers by inspection: R0 from the left intercept, R1 from the width, and C1 from the frequency at the apex. Fitting refines them; it should not be what discovers them.

2.4Why you must look at both plots

The Nyquist plot shows the shape of the response but hides the frequency, and because both axes share a scale the high-frequency end is often squeezed into a corner a few pixels wide. The Bode plots show the frequency explicitly and spread the whole range evenly, but make the shape harder to see.

Switch the explorer above to R(RC)(RC) and separate the two time constants by three decades: the Bode phase plot shows two clean peaks while the Nyquist plot shows two overlapping arcs you would struggle to count. Bring the time constants within a factor of ten of each other and both plots show a single feature, which is the honest answer: two processes that fast cannot be resolved by this measurement.

Rule of thumb from the tutorial literature: two semicircles are well resolved when their time constants differ by a factor of about 100, and merge into one when they are equal.

2.5The same spectrum from different circuits

An equivalent circuit is a hypothesis, not a measurement. Different arrangements of elements can be mathematically identical, producing spectra that cannot be told apart at any frequency. The circuit R₁(C₁R₂)(C₂R₃) and a suitably chosen nested arrangement are a classic example.

Two consequences for practice. A good fit is necessary but never sufficient evidence that your circuit is right; and the choice between two circuits that fit equally well has to be made on physical grounds, from what you know is happening at the interface, not from χ². Keep the circuit as simple as the data allow and justify every element by a process you can name.

3.1From a cell to a circuit

Nothing inside an electrochemical cell is a resistor or a capacitor. The equivalent circuit is a statement that, as far as a small sinusoid can tell, the cell responds as if it were made of these elements. Each one stands for a physical process, and the mapping is what makes the method chemistry rather than electronics.

ElementPhysical processWhere it shows up
Ruionic conduction through the electrolyte between working and reference electrodehigh-frequency intercept
Cdlcharging and discharging of the electrical double layersets the frequency of the arc apex
Rctkinetics of the electron-transfer stepdiameter of the arc
ZWdiffusion of the redox species to the surface45° tail at low frequency
Cc, Rporecapacitance of a film and ionic resistance of the paths through itsecond, higher-frequency arc
CPEa distribution of time constants across a non-uniform surfacedepressed, flattened arc

The total current through the interface splits in two, and that split is what the parallel branch in every one of these circuits expresses:

itotal=iC+iFi_{total} = i_C + i_F

One part charges the double layer, the other crosses the interface as a faradaic reaction. All of it must travel through the electrolyte, which is why Ru sits in series with everything else.

3.2The Randles circuit

Represent the faradaic branch by a general impedance ZF and you have the circuit Bard and Faulkner call the Randles equivalent circuit. ZF itself splits into a kinetic part and a transport part:

ZF=Rct+ZWZ_F = R_{ct} + Z_W

The charge-transfer resistance follows from linearising the Butler–Volmer equation about the equilibrium potential, which makes it the impedance route to the same exchange current density a Tafel plot gives:

Rct=RTnF i0=RTn2F2Ak0CR_{ct} = \frac{RT}{nF\,i_0} = \frac{RT}{n^2F^2Ak^0C}

The Warburg impedance describes semi-infinite linear diffusion. Its real and imaginary parts are equal and both fall as ω−1/2, which is what produces a line at exactly 45°:

ZW=σ ω−1/2(1−j),σ=RTn2F2A2(1DO CO∗+1DR CR∗)Z_W = \sigma\,\omega^{-1/2}(1-j), \qquad \sigma = \frac{RT}{n^2F^2A\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D_O}\,C_O^{*}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{D_R}\,C_R^{*}}\right)

Reading that σ backwards is how an impedance spectrum delivers a diffusion coefficient. Note what it contains: concentration and area as well as D, so the same cautions about electrode area apply here as anywhere else.

3.4What each system is telling you

The blocking electrode. No redox couple, so no faradaic path. The circuit is just RuCdl in series and the Nyquist plot is a vertical line rising from Ru. This is the measurement used to extract double-layer capacitance and, from it, electrochemically active area.

Kinetic control. Add a redox couple and a fast enough electrode and you get the single semicircle of Ru(CdlRct). Lower k0 and the arc grows; raise it and the arc shrinks toward the origin.

Randles with diffusion. At low frequency the perturbation is slow enough that the depletion layer has time to grow, and the 45° Warburg tail appears. Whether you see a clean semicircle before it depends on the ratio of Rct to the Warburg resistance; the arc is well separated only when Rct ≳ σ/√ω over the measured range. Push k0 above about 10−2 cm s−1 and the semicircle disappears into the tail: the system is now so fast that the spectrum carries almost no kinetic information.

The coated electrode. A film adds its own capacitance in parallel with the resistance of the electrolyte-filled pores through it, giving Ru(Cc[Rpore(CdlRct)]) and two time constants. The film is fast and appears at high frequency; the corroding metal underneath is slow and appears at low frequency. An intact coating shows only a near-vertical capacitive line, because Rpore is too large to measure.

The extra slider for the coated cases is worth dwelling on. Only the metal at the bottom of the pores is in contact with electrolyte, so the kinetics you measure belong to a small fraction of the geometric area. Both affected quantities scale with it, in opposite directions: Rct is inversely proportional to the exposed area and Cdl is proportional to it. This is why a coated sample can show an enormous charge-transfer resistance while the metal itself is perfectly ordinary, and why a capacitance measured on a coated electrode is a direct estimate of how much metal is exposed.

3.5The constant phase element

Real electrodes almost never give the perfect semicircle these equations predict. The arc is flattened, its centre sits below the real axis, and the phase maximum falls short of 90°. The usual repair replaces the ideal capacitor by a constant phase element:

ZCPE=1Q (jω)nZ_{CPE} = \frac{1}{Q\,(j\omega)^{n}}

The exponent n carries the meaning. At n = 1 the element is an ideal capacitor and Q has units of farads. At n = 0 it is a pure resistor. At n = 0.5 it is mathematically a Warburg impedance. In between, the phase is held constant at −90°n across the whole frequency range, which is where the name comes from.

A CPE is a description, not an explanation. Surface roughness, a distribution of reaction rates across the surface, porosity and slow adsorption of impurities have all been proposed as the origin, and the question is not settled. What you should report is Q and n together, since Q alone has no physical meaning, plus an effective capacitance if you need one:

Ceff=[Q(1Ru+1Rct)n−1]1/nC_{eff} = \left[Q\left(\frac{1}{R_u}+\frac{1}{R_{ct}}\right)^{n-1}\right]^{1/n}

Move the n slider in the explorer above from 1 down to 0.8 and watch the arc sink. A measured n below about 0.8 usually means the surface is genuinely heterogeneous, and the capacitance you extract from it should be treated as an estimate.

4.4Inductive loops, real and artificial

Points below the real axis are not always an artifact, and telling the two cases apart is a matter of which end of the spectrum they appear at.

At high frequency the loop of cable between the potentiostat and the cell has inductance of the order of a microhenry per metre. Its reactance grows with frequency, so above a few kilohertz it adds a positive imaginary part that pulls the spectrum below the axis. It is invisible on a megohm cell and ruinous on a milliohm one, which is why battery and fuel-cell work demands short, twisted, shielded leads.

At low frequency an inductive loop is usually real chemistry: an adsorbed intermediate whose coverage relaxes on the timescale of the perturbation. Iron dissolving in sulfuric acid shows exactly this through the adsorbed FeOH intermediate, and the loop is diagnostic of the mechanism rather than a nuisance.

4.5Fitting, by hand

Choose a dataset, choose a model, and move the sliders until the curves overlap and the residuals are flat and scattered about zero. There is no automatic fit here on purpose: driving the parameters yourself is what teaches which one owns which feature of the spectrum.

Judge the fit on the residuals, never on the Nyquist overlay. The overlay is dominated by the largest impedances and will look convincing while the model is wrong; the residual plot will show a smooth curve with frequency instead of noise. The chi-squared value quoted by fitting software is the sum of squared relative residuals, and values below about 10−4 are usually acceptable.

4.6Before you believe any of it

A spectrum is only interpretable if the three conditions from tab 1 held while it was recorded. The Kramers–Kronig relations connect the real and imaginary parts of any linear, causal, stable response, so a spectrum that fails to transform is a spectrum that violated one of them. In practice the test is run by fitting a Voigt circuit, a long chain of (RC) elements with fixed time constants, which can fit anything that is Kramers–Kronig transformable and nothing that is not.

Practical checklist

  • Amplitude. 5 to 10 mV peak unless you have shown the response is still linear. Check whether a literature value is quoted as peak, peak-to-peak or rms: 10 mV peak is 20 mV peak-to-peak and 7 mV rms.
  • Frequency range. Wide enough to cover every time constant present. 100 kHz down to 10 mHz covers most aqueous work; solid electrolytes need megahertz, coatings need millihertz.
  • Time. One cycle at 1 mHz lasts 1000 s, so a few averaged cycles at the bottom of the sweep cost most of an hour. Ask whether your sample is still the same sample by then.
  • Stationarity. Record the open-circuit potential before and after. If it moved, the spectrum is not of one state.
  • Connections. Short, shielded, twisted leads; cage closed and grounded at one point; nothing else switching on and off nearby.
  • Validation. Run the Kramers–Kronig test before interpreting anything, and inspect the residuals rather than χ² alone.
  • Reporting. Give the circuit, every fitted parameter with its error, the χ², and the physical justification for each element. For a CPE report Q and n together, never Q alone.

4.7Check your understanding

A Nyquist plot looks like a perfect semicircle, but the phase maximum is only 68°. What does that tell you?

That the arc is depressed, and the eye cannot see it. An ideal parallel RC combination approaches −90° when the resistances are well separated; a constant phase element caps the phase near 90°n, so 68° suggests n ≈ 0.76. Fit with a CPE and report n. Never judge ideality from the Nyquist shape alone.

Two spectra have the same Rct but their arcs peak at different frequencies. What differs?

The capacitance, since the apex sits at ω = 1/RctCdl. A larger Cdl at the same Rct means more active area or a rougher surface, which is also why effective capacitance is used to estimate electrochemically active area.

Your spectrum dives below the real axis above 10 kHz. Is that chemistry?

Almost certainly not. At the high-frequency end this is the inductance of the cable loop, and it scales with lead length. Shorten and twist the leads and the loop shrinks. An inductive loop at the low-frequency end is a different matter and usually indicates a relaxing adsorbed intermediate.

Why can a fit with a low χ² still be wrong?

Because χ² measures distance, not structure. A model missing an element often produces residuals that curve smoothly with frequency while remaining small. Systematic residuals mean the circuit is wrong no matter how small they are. Beyond that, several circuits can be mathematically identical, so fit quality alone can never select between them.

You need the spectrum down to 1 mHz on a sample that corrodes visibly in an hour. What do you do?

Accept that you cannot. One cycle at 1 mHz is 1000 s, and a few averaged cycles put you past the hour, during which the system is no longer stationary and the Kramers–Kronig conditions are violated. Either stop at a higher frequency, slow the system down, or record a sequence of faster spectra and report the evolution.

A blocking electrode should give a vertical line. Yours is tilted by 15°. What do you report?

A CPE rather than a capacitance. The tilt is 90°(1 − n), so 15° corresponds to n ≈ 0.83. Report Q and n, and if you need a capacitance for an area estimate, convert it with the appropriate effective-capacitance formula and say which one you used.
